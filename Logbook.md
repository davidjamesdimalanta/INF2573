### Starting with a product direction

Our first team decision was to explore an opportunity in the food community through fridge inventory tracking. We started with that direction, created a North Star, and then built opportunity trees to identify what we needed to test. This made me reflect on the difference between approaching a project as a UX designer and approaching it with the product thinking of a startup founder.

My starting view is that UX design works from the bottom up. I get to know people and their needs, identify problems, and look for opportunities to address them. Our process felt more top down because we began with a broad product direction and worked toward specific opportunities and tests. I see this as a difference in where we started, rather than a fixed boundary between the two roles. In either approach, we need to return to users frequently and be willing to change direction.

### Connecting the framework to our decision

Our [North Star exercise](artifacts/entry-01/north-star-exercise.png) framed the goal as minimizing food waste and helping people use ingredients in their fridge in a fun way. We considered several measures, including items saved per week, meals cooked, and the dollar value of food saved. The exercise connected that goal to reducing logging effort, making freshness visible, and making it easier to use remaining ingredients. Receipt scanning, a colour-coded fridge interface, and recipe recommendations were possible interventions.

The [opportunity solution tree](artifacts/entry-01/opportunity-solution-tree.md) makes the working outcome more specific: increasing the number of logged items marked as used before expiry each week. This gives us something to test, but it also exposes an important limitation. Marking an item as used does not prove that the app prevented it from being wasted. Someone might have used it anyway. I need to distinguish a convenient measure of activity from evidence that we achieved our goal.

The tree also complicates my initial description of product thinking. We chose a direction from the top down, but the opportunities still need support from people's experiences. The artifact records stories about cheese being pushed to the back of a fridge and partly used cottage cheese being forgotten after a household routine changed. Those stories support investigating visibility and remembering remaining food. They do not establish that people want receipt scanning, notifications, or recipe recommendations.

### What I am learning about my role

My reflection is about how I move between understanding a person's problem and deciding what a product should achieve. Starting with a direction helps organize the questions, but I can also see how quickly a proposed feature can begin to feel necessary. Fridge inventory tracking already suggests a solution before we know whether people will keep an inventory current.

For me, the useful discipline is to keep asking which parts of our concept come from people's experiences and which parts are still our assumptions. I want to carry that question into how I contribute to the team's next iteration.

### Wider implications and human agency

Our metric could encourage us to ask households to record more information because that makes our product easier to measure. That would place the work of keeping the inventory accurate on the people we are trying to help. The interview account about not having time to prepare fruit makes me question whether another recording task would fit their routines.

I also need to consider what our definition of success leaves out. Counting items saved would not, by itself, capture the effort required or whether people feel pressured by reminders. If we use AI to interpret receipts or recommend meals, people should be able to correct its suggestions and decide what to use. Our goal of reducing waste should leave room for their preferences, time constraints, and judgment.

### Uncertainty and the next test

The tree reports one group interview, with two transcripts of the same recording. It is a starting point for identifying questions, rather than evidence that these needs are widespread. It also records a shift from targeting logging effort without interview evidence to investigating how people notice seldom-used food. That shift shows how research can change which part of our original direction deserves attention.

The proposed first test compares a fridge screen that puts use-soon items first with an unsorted view. I would look for whether people notice a forgotten item and can choose an action, while asking whether they trust the information. This would test the interface's usefulness in that moment; it would not yet demonstrate reduced waste over time.

The physical “use first” area proposed in the tree is an equally useful challenge to our concept. If changing where food sits helps people remember it with less effort, we need to consider what value an app adds. Returning to users should allow us to question fridge tracking itself, as well as improve its interface. These tests are proposed and have not been reported as completed.
